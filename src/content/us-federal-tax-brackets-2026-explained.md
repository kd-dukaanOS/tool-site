**Think earning $100,000 means paying 22% in federal income tax? It doesn't.**

The U.S. uses a **progressive federal income tax system** — different portions of your taxable income are taxed at different rates. In this guide, you'll see the 2026 federal tax brackets, how marginal tax rates actually work, and exactly how the math plays out for a single filer earning $100,000. Want to skip the tax math? ## 2026 Federal Tax Brackets for Single Filers

| Federal Tax Rate | Taxable Income |
| ----------------- | --------------: |
| 10%                |    $0 – $12,400 |
| 12%                |  $12,401 – $50,400 |
| 22%                | $50,401 – $105,700 |
| 24%                | $105,701 – $201,775 |
| 32%                | $201,776 – $256,225 |
| 35%                | $256,226 – $640,600 |
| 37%                |    Over $640,600 |

Remember: these are **marginal brackets based on taxable income**, not your gross salary.

## The Most Important Thing to Understand About Tax Brackets

*"I'm in the 22% bracket, so I pay 22% tax."* **No.** You pay different rates on different portions of your taxable income. Think of the brackets like a series of buckets — your first dollars fill the 10% bucket, then the 12% bucket, then the 22% bucket, and so on. Moving into a higher bracket never means your *entire* income gets taxed at that higher rate.

## Example: A Single Person Earning $100,000

**Gross salary: $100,000**

**− 2026 standard deduction: $16,100**

**= Taxable income: $83,900**

| Portion of taxable income | Rate | Tax |
| -------------------------- | ---: | ---: |
| First $12,400               | 10%  | $1,240 |
| Next $38,000                 | 12%  | $4,560 |
| Remaining $33,500            | 22%  | $7,370 |
| **Total**                    |      | **$13,170** |

**Estimated federal income tax: $13,170** — about **13.17%** of the original $100,000 salary, even though the marginal bracket is 22%.

## What Is the 2026 Standard Deduction?

The standard deduction reduces the amount of income used to calculate federal income tax.

| Filing Status             | 2026 Standard Deduction |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------: |
| Single                      |                 $16,100 |
| Married Filing Separately   |                 $16,100 |

## A Quick Tax-Bracket Myth Test

### Myth #1: "If I enter the 22% bracket, all my income is taxed at 22%."
**False.** Only the portion within that bracket is taxed at 22%.

### Myth #2: "A higher tax bracket means I could take home less after a raise."
Usually false — only the income reaching the higher bracket is taxed at the higher rate.

### Myth #3: "My salary is my taxable income."
Not necessarily — deductions reduce taxable income below your salary.

### Myth #4: "Federal income tax is the only tax coming out of my paycheck."
No — payroll taxes and potentially state/local taxes also apply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the 2026 federal tax brackets?
For single filers: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%, applied to different portions of taxable income.

### What is the 2026 standard deduction for a single person?
$16,100.

### What tax bracket is $100,000 in for 2026?
About $83,900 taxable income after the standard deduction — placing it in the 22% marginal bracket.

### Does being in the 22% bracket mean I pay 22% on everything?
No — the system is progressive.

### What is the highest federal tax rate in 2026?
37%, above $640,600 (single) or $768,700 (married filing jointly) of taxable income.

### Is take-home pay the same as taxable income?
No. Taxable income determines your tax bill; take-home pay is what's left after taxes and other payroll deductions.

### Is $100,000 a good salary in the US?
It can provide a strong income in many parts of the country, but how much you actually keep depends on federal, payroll and state/local taxes, benefits, retirement contributions, filing status, and where you live.

### What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?
Your marginal rate applies to your next dollar of taxable income. Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your total income — almost always lower than your marginal rate.

## The Bottom Line

**Your highest tax bracket is not the same thing as your overall tax rate.** For a single filer: $100,000 gross → $16,100 standard deduction → $83,900 taxable income → about $13,170 federal income tax, before credits.
