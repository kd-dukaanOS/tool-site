Two deductions appear on almost every Canadian paycheque — CPP and EI — but most people never see exactly how the numbers are calculated.

Both are mandatory payroll deductions for most employees, but they serve very different purposes. CPP helps provide retirement, disability and survivor benefits, while Employment Insurance (EI) provides temporary income support for eligible workers. And neither is charged on your entire salary forever — both have annual earnings limits, and CPP has an additional contribution tier for higher earners.

**Important:** The calculations in this article are for employees generally covered by the Canada Pension Plan (CPP) and the federal EI system. Quebec has its own Quebec Pension Plan (QPP), and Quebec employees have a different EI premium rate.

## 2026 CPP and EI Rates at a Glance

| Program | Employee Rate | 2026 Earnings Limit | Max Employee Contribution |
| ------- | -------------: | -------------------- | -------------------------: |
| CPP — base + first additional | 5.95%* | Up to $74,600 | $4,230.45 |
| CPP2 — second additional | 4.00% | $74,600 – $85,000 | $416.00 |
| EI | 1.63% | Up to $68,900 | $1,123.07 |

*The 5.95% figure is the combined base CPP and first additional CPP rate — base CPP is 4.95%, with the first additional contribution adding 1%.

## How Is CPP Calculated?

CPP isn't charged on every dollar of your salary. The first $3,500 of annual pensionable earnings is exempt, and contributions apply up to the $74,600 first earnings ceiling (YMPE) at 5.95%. Higher earners have an additional layer on top: CPP2.

## Worked Example: $50,000 Salary

**Gross salary: $50,000**

**Pensionable earnings: $50,000 − $3,500 = $46,500**

**CPP: $46,500 × 5.95% = $2,766.75**

Because $50,000 is below the $74,600 first CPP ceiling, CPP2 does not apply.

**EI: $50,000 × 1.63% = $815**

| Deduction | Estimated amount |
| --------- | ------------------: |
| CPP | $2,766.75 |
| EI | $815.00 |
| **Total** | **$3,581.75** |

## Worked Example: $80,000 Salary (Where CPP2 Kicks In)

At $80,000, your earnings exceed the $74,600 first CPP ceiling, so CPP2 applies to the portion above it.

## What Is CPP2?

CPP2 is the second additional CPP contribution for higher earners, introduced as part of the CPP enhancement. For 2026: it applies at 4% to earnings between $74,600 and $85,000, capped at $416. Employers match your CPP contribution dollar-for-dollar, including CPP2 — so someone earning $85,000 or more reaches a maximum total employee CPP contribution of **$4,230.45 + $416 = $4,646.45**.

## Worked Example: $100,000 Salary

At $100,000, you've exceeded both CPP ceilings and the EI maximum — every deduction is at its cap.

| Deduction | Annual amount |
| --------- | --------------: |
| Base + first additional CPP | $4,230.45 |
| CPP2 | $416.00 |
| EI | $1,123.07 |
| **CPP + EI total** | **$5,769.52** |

## Why EI Stops Increasing After $68,900

Once your insurable earnings reach $68,900, you've hit the maximum employee EI premium of $1,123.07 for the year — someone earning $70,000, $100,000 or $200,000 doesn't keep paying 1.63% on every extra dollar. CPP is different: CPP2 creates an additional band between $74,600 and $85,000, so CPP contributions can still increase a little further than EI's.

## Do Employers Pay CPP and EI Too?

Yes, and separately from what's deducted from your pay. Employers match your full CPP contribution (including CPP2), and pay 1.4× your EI premium — up to a maximum employer EI premium of $1,572.30 per employee for 2026.

## CPP vs. EI: What's the Difference?

- **CPP** funds your future retirement pension (plus disability and survivor benefits) — it's really a forced savings program, the money comes back to you later.
- **EI** funds temporary income support for job loss or leave — it's insurance, not savings.

## A Quick CPP & EI Myth Test

### Myth #1: "CPP and EI are the same thing."
**False.** CPP is retirement/disability/survivor benefits; EI is temporary income support.

### Myth #2: "I pay 5.95% CPP on my entire salary."
**False.** CPP uses a $3,500 basic exemption and an earnings ceiling — and higher earners are also subject to CPP2.

### Myth #3: "CPP stops at $4,230.45 for everyone."
Not quite — that's the maximum for base CPP alone. Anyone earning into the CPP2 range can contribute up to $416 more, for a combined maximum of $4,646.45.

### Myth #4: "High earners keep paying EI forever."
**False.** EI has a maximum insurable earnings limit of $68,900, capping the employee premium at $1,123.07.

### Myth #5: "Self-employed Canadians don't pay CPP."
**False.** Self-employed people generally pay both the employee and employer portions — 11.9% total for base CPP, before CPP2.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the CPP contribution rate for 2026?
5.95% combined (base + first additional), on pensionable earnings between the $3,500 exemption and the $74,600 first ceiling.

### What is the maximum CPP contribution for 2026?
$4,230.45 for base CPP, plus up to $416 more from CPP2 — a combined maximum of $4,646.45.

### What is CPP2?
An additional 4% CPP contribution on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000, capped at $416.

### What is the EI premium rate for 2026?
1.63% for employees outside Quebec, up to $68,900 of insurable earnings.

### What is the maximum EI premium for 2026?
$1,123.07 for employees; $1,572.30 for employers.

### Is the EI rate the same in Quebec?
No — Quebec's 2026 employee EI rate is 1.30%, with a maximum premium of $895.70, because Quebec runs its own parental insurance program.

### Is CPP the same as QPP?
No — Quebec operates its own Quebec Pension Plan (QPP) with different rates; the CPP examples here don't apply directly to Quebec workers.

### Does this article calculate my exact payroll deductions?
No — these are simplified annual calculations. Actual deductions depend on pay frequency, employment status, and other payroll circumstances. For your own estimate, use the [CPP Calculator](/finance/cpp-calculator/) or [EI Calculator](/finance/ei-calculator/).

## The Bottom Line

CPP and EI aren't flat taxes on your entire salary — both cap out. For 2026: the base CPP maximum is $4,230.45, CPP2 can add up to $416 more, and EI caps at $1,123.07. A $50,000 salary sees about $2,766.75 of CPP and $815 of EI; by $100,000, all three are maxed out at a combined $5,769.52.
