## 2026/27 National Insurance Rates for Employees

For most employees paying **Class 1 National Insurance**, the main 2026/27 rates are:

| Band                                      | Annual Earnings | Employee NI Rate |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------: | ---------------: |
| Below Primary Threshold                   |   Under £12,570 |           **0%** |
| Primary Threshold to Upper Earnings Limit | £12,570–£50,270 |           **8%** |
| Above Upper Earnings Limit                |    Over £50,270 |           **2%** |

So, under the standard employee calculation, there is no employee NI on earnings below **£12,570**. Earnings between **£12,570 and £50,270** are subject to an 8% rate, while earnings above **£50,270** are subject to the lower 2% rate.

**Important:** These are employee National Insurance rates. Employer National Insurance is calculated separately.

## How Does National Insurance Work?

The easiest way to understand employee National Insurance is to think of your earnings as moving through different bands.

Your earnings don't all get charged at the same rate.

For example, if you earn **£60,000**, you don't pay 8% on the entire amount above £12,570.

Instead:

* The portion from **£12,570 to £50,270** is subject to 8%.
* The portion above **£50,270** is subject to 2%.

That means earning more can actually result in a **lower marginal National Insurance rate on your additional earnings** once you pass the Upper Earnings Limit.

# Worked Example: £30,000 Salary

Let's assume a standard employee calculation with no circumstances that change the normal Class 1 NI calculation.

### Gross salary

**£30,000**

### Earnings above the Primary Threshold

**£30,000 − £12,570 = £17,430**

### National Insurance

**£17,430 × 8% = £1,394.40**

### Estimated employee NI

## **£1,394.40 per year**

That's approximately:

**£116.20 per month**

Because £30,000 is below the £50,270 Upper Earnings Limit, all of the earnings above the £12,570 Primary Threshold fall within the 8% band.

# Worked Example: £50,000 Salary

A £50,000 salary is still below the Upper Earnings Limit of £50,270.

### Earnings subject to NI

**£50,000 − £12,570 = £37,430**

### Employee NI

**£37,430 × 8% = £2,994.40**

### Estimated employee NI

## **£2,994.40 per year**

That's approximately **£249.53 per month**.

The entire amount above the Primary Threshold remains within the 8% employee NI band.

---

# Worked Example: £60,000 Salary

This is where the calculation becomes more interesting.

At £60,000, you **do not** pay 8% on everything above £12,570.

The calculation splits into two bands:

| Portion of earnings       | Rate |   Employee NI |
| ------------------------- | ---: | ------------: |
| £12,570–£50,270 (£37,700) |   8% |        £3,016 |
| £50,270–£60,000 (£9,730)  |   2% |       £194.60 |
| **Total**                 |      | **£3,210.60** |

### Estimated employee NI

## **£3,210.60 per year**

That's approximately **£267.55 per month**.

Notice what happens: someone earning £60,000 does **not** pay twice as much employee NI as someone earning £30,000.

Once earnings pass £50,270, the **marginal employee NI rate falls from 8% to 2%**.

# What Happens to National Insurance on a £100,000 Salary?

The same principle applies to higher salaries.

For a simplified £100,000 employee salary:

| Portion of earnings | Rate |
| ------------------- | ---: |
| £12,570–£50,270     |   8% |
| £50,270–£100,000    |   2% |

The calculation is:

**£37,700 × 8% = £3,016**

**£49,730 × 2% = £994.60**

### Estimated employee NI

## **£4,010.60 per year**

That's approximately **£334.22 per month**.

The important point is that the additional earnings above £50,270 are subject to the lower **2% employee NI rate**, not 8%.

# Don't Forget: Your Employer Pays National Insurance Too

Employee National Insurance and employer National Insurance are separate.

Your employer generally pays **Class 1 secondary National Insurance** on qualifying earnings above the Secondary Threshold.

For 2026/27, the main employer rate is **15%**, with a **£5,000 Secondary Threshold**.

Unlike employee National Insurance, employer NI does not switch to a lower 2% rate above £50,270.

For a straightforward £30,000 salary example, assuming the standard employer NI rules apply:

**£30,000 − £5,000 = £25,000**

**£25,000 × 15% = £3,750**

So the employer's National Insurance contribution would be:

### **£3,750**

This is separate from the employee's £1,394.40 NI contribution.

Employer NI is one reason an employee's salary is **not the same as their total cost to the employer**.

# National Insurance vs Income Tax: What's the Difference?

National Insurance and Income Tax can both affect your pay, but they are separate systems with different rules and thresholds.

### Income Tax

Income Tax is charged on taxable income using different tax bands. It helps fund general government spending.

### National Insurance

National Insurance contributions help fund social security entitlements and public services, including the State Pension and NHS-related spending. They also contribute toward certain contributory benefits.

The two systems use different calculations.

For 2026/27, the standard employee National Insurance Primary Threshold is **£12,570**, which is also the standard Income Tax Personal Allowance for many taxpayers.

However, reaching the same threshold does **not** mean Income Tax and National Insurance are calculated in exactly the same way.

# A Quick National Insurance Myth Test

## Myth #1: "I pay 8% National Insurance on my whole salary."

**False.**

Under the standard employee calculation, there is no employee NI below £12,570, earnings between £12,570 and £50,270 are subject to 8%, and earnings above £50,270 are subject to 2%.

---

## Myth #2: "Earning more means my National Insurance rate goes up."

Not necessarily.

Once earnings pass £50,270, the **marginal employee NI rate on additional earnings falls from 8% to 2%**.

---

## Myth #3: "My employer's National Insurance comes out of my salary."

**No.**

Employer National Insurance is a separate employer cost and is not deducted from your gross salary as employee NI.

---

## Myth #4: "National Insurance and Income Tax are the same thing."

**No.**

They are separate systems with different rates, thresholds and rules.

## Does National Insurance apply to Scotland the same way?

**Yes.**

National Insurance is a UK-wide system, so the main employee NI rates and thresholds apply across **England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland**.

Scotland has different Income Tax bands, but Scottish Income Tax and National Insurance are separate systems.

---

## What does National Insurance actually pay for?

National Insurance contributions help support social security entitlements and public services. They contribute toward areas including the State Pension and certain contributory benefits, while National Insurance revenue also contributes to NHS-related spending.

Your entitlement to some benefits, including the State Pension, can depend on your National Insurance record and qualifying years.

---

## Is National Insurance the same as Income Tax?

No.

Income Tax and National Insurance are separate charges with different rules, rates and thresholds.

That's why knowing your Income Tax band alone isn't enough to work out how much will be deducted from your pay.
